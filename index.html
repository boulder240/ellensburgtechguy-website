For small law offices

Every hour on admin is an hour you can’t bill.

Small practices run on the same handful of systems: case management, document assembly, e-filing, email, phone and calendar. Almost none of them talk to each other out of the box. So somebody retypes. Somebody re-files. Somebody checks a voicemail box at 8am that filled up at 6pm.

These are the exact automations and systems I have been building for the last ten years: connecting filing portals to case management systems, wiring document and email workflows together, and getting phone and database systems to hand information to each other instead of relying on somebody to retype it. The same problems show up in a two-attorney practice as in a large office, just smaller.

Why your obligations make this different

Washington adopted ABA Comment 8 to RPC 1.1 verbatim, effective 1 January 2014. Competence includes keeping abreast of “the benefits and risks associated with relevant technology.” Add your confidentiality duties on top and generic small-business IT advice stops being good enough. Convenience that leaks privileged material is a liability, not an efficiency. Source: Washington Courts, RPC 1.1. Verify against current rules before relying on any summary.
Intake that retypes itselfA form the client fills in once, landing in your case management system already populated. No transcription, no second version of the truth.
Document assemblyEngagement letters, standard pleadings and routine correspondence generated from matter data rather than the last similar file plus find-and-replace.
E-filing and court portalsAssembling filing packets, uploading, and getting confirmations and stamped copies back into the right matter folder without manual downloads.
Docketing and deadlinesCourt dates and statutory deadlines calculated once and pushed to the calendar, with reminders that fire early enough to matter.
Email that files itself by matterRules and shared mailboxes so correspondence lands against the case instead of living in one person’s inbox until they are out of the office.
Phone that captures the callAfter-hours routing, voicemail transcribed to email, and new-client calls logged rather than lost. A missed intake call is the most expensive thing in the office.
Scanning and searchable filesOCR so a scanned exhibit is findable by its contents, and a naming convention everyone in the office actually follows.
Backups and access controlEncrypted, tested backups and permissions that reflect who should actually see a matter. The unglamorous part that protects the rest.
Illustrative example

Intake typed twice, then typed again

A two-attorney practice takes new client details on paper, retypes them into case management, then retypes parts again into the engagement letter. Around four hours a week between two staff, and the three versions do not always agree.

Replaced with one intake form feeding both. Roughly a day a month back, and one source of truth.

Illustrative example

Filings assembled by hand

Every filing means gathering documents, combining them, uploading through the portal, then downloading the conformed copy and dragging it into the right folder. Ten to fifteen minutes each, several times a day.

Automated end to end. Minutes per filing, not a quarter hour, and the copy is always filed correctly.

Illustrative example

The 5pm call nobody hears

Someone calls at 5:40pm looking for representation, reaches voicemail, and calls the next firm on the list before anyone checks the box the following morning.

Routed and transcribed to email on arrival. One retained client pays for the setup several times over.

These are illustrations of the kind of work, not client testimonials. I would rather show you the arithmetic on your own office than quote someone else’s.
